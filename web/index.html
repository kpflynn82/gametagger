Next · Play-testing

When the store page runs out, it plays the game

Store pages show what a game wants you to see. The questions product and monetization teams ask most only show up after hours of play: how often ads interrupt, when an energy timer stops a session, what it costs to keep progressing. So an AI player plays the game on an Android emulator. It taps and swipes like a person, writes notes on every screen, and measures what the store page couldn't show.

Pacing you can measure

Ads per round and per hour, minutes between interruptions, and how long a session runs before a timer ends it. Store pages never show these.

An economy you can see

What each upgrade costs, how fast coins come in, and the point where progress slows until you watch an ad or pay.

Why it works

The core loop, step by step, and which systems carry the fun and which only extract: a keep, drop or replace list for anyone building in the genre.

From play-test to playable prototype. The notes from this game became the brief for Fold & Fly, a playable prototype of the genre: first playable overnight, in a web browser, with bot players testing its pacing (a forced ad after 1 in 9 flights in simulated play, against about 1 in 1 in the original). Teams can find out whether a feature is fun in days, before they staff it.

Case study: a dice-board game, rebuilt as Hoard & Hatch

From an AI play-test to a game you can play in your browser

The AI player played a top-grossing dice-board game as a guest, logging every tutorial screen and where the coins came from. One finding shaped the prototype: after the first city, most of the money came from lump-sum events, not from landing on spaces. In one later city, the run logs show about 26,000 coins from landings and about 584,000 from lump sums (rough figures).

That play-test became the brief for Hoard & Hatch: the same roll, earn and build loop with its own theme. You raise a dragon that grows up as you rebuild two realms, put houses on board spaces, and raid rival dragons' towns.

▶ Play Hoard & HatchWorks on a phone or a desktop browser

How it was built in about a day

Two AI coding agents, one person directing and merging their work

  1. Play. The AI player records the new-player flow screen by screen and measures the economy.
  2. Brief. Claude turns the notes into a design brief: keep the core loop, change the theme, list what to test.
  3. First playable. Claude builds the 3D board, dice, economy, tutorial and a tuning panel, with a simulator that estimates rolls per realm.
  4. Art and phone UI. Codex generates the illustrated art and reworks the screens for phones.
  5. Mechanics. Claude adds houses on spaces, raids on rival towns and the dragon's growth. The person directing picks between design options.
  6. Polish. Codex draws the older dragon forms and names the streets.

About a day from the first commit to this build. Each agent works on its own branch, and automated checks play the tutorial at phone sizes after every change. One game, built as a pilot; not yet tested with players.

How it works

Describe first, then decide

GameTagger in 82 seconds

A silent film of the whole method, with this page's own numbers, then what comes next: play a game, learn what makes it work, and build a playable prototype.

  1. The NitroGen dataset's game list (40,000 hours of gameplay, 1,000+ games) seeded the catalog.
  2. 189 gameplay tags and 100 genres, each defined with the evidence allowed to prove it.
  3. Claude describes screenshots and video. Jev decides, one question per tag.
  4. Everything lands here.
  5. When evidence runs out, an AI player plays the game: 13 hours of a slingshot plane game, every forced ad counted.
  6. It works out what makes the game work, and what to keep, drop or replace.
  7. Then it builds a playable prototype, shown in real footage.

Claude describes

Claude looks at each screenshot and six short trailer clips and writes down what is visible: a first-person view of a rifle, a timer, a shop screen. It is not allowed to name genres or tags.

Jev decides

Jev, a classification model from TypeSafe, answers each of 189 tag questions on its own, using only the evidence each tag allows. It never sees the game's name, so it can't lean on reputation.

Unknown stays unknown

Every tag is present, absent, conflicting or not enough evidence, with a probability. A missing tag never quietly becomes a "no", and every tag keeps a record of the evidence behind it.
